# Voice and tone for jermy.design

The site is a design leader's portfolio. The words should sound like Luke talking
to a peer across a table: plain, confident, a little wry, never selling.

## The voice

**Plain-spoken.** Short, common words. Say the thing directly.
**Confident, not boastful.** Let the work and the facts carry it. No "passionate",
"world-class", "rockstar", "synergy".
**A bit wry.** Light humour where it's earned ("Head of design. Still doing the
doing."), never at the expense of clarity, and never jokey in functional copy.
**Human.** First person ("I"), contractions ("I've", "don't"), people over process.

On NN/g's four tone dimensions, the site sits at: casual (not formal), serious with
flashes of humour (not funny), respectful (not irreverent), matter-of-fact (not
enthusiastic). Hold those across pages; shift tone by context, not voice.

### Reference copy (the bar to match)

- Hero: "Head of design. Still doing the doing."
- Work heading: "From early-stage startups to enterprise tech"
- Values: "People are always the hardest part", "Difficult questions from a place of
  love", "When the learning stops, change it up"
- Case short lines: "Ethical banking, from zero to MVP", "Measuring impact for
  philanthropy"

## UX writing fundamentals

Grounded in Nielsen Norman Group's research on how people read online, plus the
GOV.UK style guide.

1. **People scan, they don't read.** Most visitors read a fraction of the words
   (NN/g *How Users Read on the Web*). Write for scanning: clear headings, short
   paragraphs (two or three sentences), one idea each.
2. **Concise.** Cut every word that doesn't add meaning. NN/g found concise,
   scannable, objective copy measurably more usable than promotional copy.
   Common cuts: "many different" → "many", "in order to" → "to", "really",
   "very", "just", "actually".
3. **Front-load.** Most important information first: inverted pyramid for
   paragraphs, keywords first in headings, links and list items, because readers
   scan in an F-pattern and the first two words of a line get the most attention.
4. **Plain language, even for experts.** Experts prefer it too (NN/g *Plain
   Language Is for Everyone, Even Experts*). Avoid jargon and acronyms unless the
   audience uses them daily; explain them once if needed.
5. **Objective over promotional.** Facts, outcomes and specifics beat adjectives.
   "Led a squad of six from discovery to launch in three months" beats
   "delivered an amazing product experience".
6. **Active voice, concrete verbs.** "I led the redesign", not "The redesign was
   led by me".
7. **Parallel structure.** Items in a list or series share a grammatical shape:
   "leading design teams in-house, consulting, and running my own design studio",
   not "leading…, working as…, and as the founder of…".
8. **Descriptive links and buttons.** Say where they go or what they do ("View the
   Vemi case study", "Book a chat"); start with the keyword. Never "Click here" or
   a bare "Learn more" when the context isn't obvious.
9. **Consistent words for the same thing.** Pick one term and keep it (case study
   vs. project vs. work: be deliberate and stay consistent).
10. **Errors and empty states.** Say what happened in plain words, why if it
    helps, and what to do next. No blame, no codes, no jokes in errors.
11. **Accessible.** Meaningful alt text (what the image shows and why it's there;
    empty `alt=""` for decoration); link text that makes sense out of context;
    don't rely on colour or position alone ("the green button").

## House style

- **British English**: colour, organisation, centre, -ise endings.
- **Sentence case** for headings, buttons and labels. No title case, no full
  stops on headings, except for deliberate statements like the hero.
- **No em dashes** (— or –) anywhere in site copy, including case studies and
  Dato content. They read as a tell of AI-written text. Rewrite instead: a
  full stop and a new sentence, a colon before an explanation or list, a
  comma for a light aside, or brackets. Don't just swap in an en dash or
  hyphen. (Em dashes in code comments are fine.)
- **Numbers as numerals** (20 years, 3 months); thousands with commas (5,652).
- **Hyphenate compound modifiers** before a noun: early-stage startups,
  long-term thinking.
- **Oxford comma** only where it prevents ambiguity.
- **Short lines on case study cards**: six words or fewer, no full stop, says
  what the product is or did. The full sentence lives on the case study page.
- **Greeting badge**: "Good morning, Copenhagen." Time of day plus city, nothing
  else.

## Review checklist

Before shipping copy, check:

- [ ] Could a visitor get the point from the heading and first sentence alone?
- [ ] Any word that could go without losing meaning? Cut it.
- [ ] Lists and series parallel?
- [ ] Active voice, concrete verbs, no hype adjectives?
- [ ] Jargon or acronyms the visitor might not know?
- [ ] Links and buttons say what they do?
- [ ] British spelling, sentence case, no em dashes?
- [ ] Reads aloud like Luke talking, not a brochure?
